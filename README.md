## Overview

Spontaneous human combustion (SHC) is the popular name for a small set of reported death cases in which a body is severely or almost completely consumed by fire — frequently leaving the torso reduced to ash while the extremities and the immediate surroundings show comparatively little fire damage. The classic case profile, first noted in early 19th-century medical literature, describes an elderly, often alcoholic, often immobile victim found alone near a fireplace, candle, or smoking material. Mainstream forensic science, supported by experimental work and case-by-case review, attributes such deaths to the 'wick effect': a small external ignition source chars clothing and skin, releases subcutaneous body fat, and the fat-soaked clothing burns slowly like a candle wick for many hours, locally consuming the body while the temperature elsewhere in the room remains low. Investigators including Joe Nickell and John F. Fischer have argued that an external ignition source is identifiable in essentially every well-documented case once the published account is checked against the forensic record. SHC nonetheless retains a strong cultural footprint, anchored by Charles Dickens's depiction of Mr. Krook's death in Bleak House (1852–53) and a small number of widely-reprinted cases from the 18th to the 21st century.

## Notable Sightings

### Death of Countess Cornelia Bandi ⭐
> **Date**: 1731-04-04  
> **Location**: Bandi family residence  
> **Witnesses**: 1 (Maidservant who discovered the body)  
The 62-year-old countess was found in the morning by her maid as a heap of ashes on the bedroom floor with her legs, intact in stockings, and skull lying nearby. Surrounding furnishings were largely undamaged. The case was investigated and reported in Latin by Giuseppe Bianchini, then translated into English by Paul Rolli in 1746.
**Credibility**: low — Foundational European case for the SHC literature.
**Sources**: Rolli, P. (1746), Phil. Trans. R. Soc. 43

---

### Mary Reeser case ⭐
> **Date**: 1951-07-02  
> **Location**: Apartment at 1200 Cherry Street NE  
> **Witnesses**: 1 (Landlady (Pansy Carpenter))  
Mary Reeser, 67, was found the morning after taking sleeping pills and smoking a cigarette in her armchair. The chair, Reeser's torso, and one slipper-clad foot remained; the rest of her body was reduced to ash. The FBI investigation concluded a dropped cigarette ignited her clothing, with body fat sustaining combustion through the wick effect.
**Credibility**: medium — The most heavily-cited 20th-century SHC case.
**Sources**: FBI Reading Room files; Nickell & Fischer (1984)

---

### Michael Faherty case ⭐
> **Date**: 2010-12-22  
> **Location**: Home on Clareview Park, Ballybane  
> **Witnesses**: 1 (Neighbour who alerted emergency services)  
Faherty, 76, was found burned in his living room in front of an open fireplace; the fire damage was confined to his body and a small area of floor and ceiling above him. After investigation, West Galway coroner Ciaran McLoughlin returned a verdict of 'death by spontaneous combustion' in September 2011, the first such verdict in modern Irish or British inquest records.
**Credibility**: medium — Frequently cited by both proponents and skeptics.
**Sources**: BBC News (2011-09-23); Galway coroner's report (2011)

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Pyrokinesis / poltergeist hypothesis** _(Michael Harrison)_

SHC reflects an unexplained internal or psychic ignition mechanism, sometimes linked to poltergeist phenomena.

### Scientific

**Wick effect** _(John de Haan, Joe Nickell, John F. Fischer)_

A small external flame ignites clothing, which chars and splits the skin, releasing subcutaneous fat that soaks the cloth and burns at low temperature for hours, consuming the body while leaving the room largely intact.

**Acetone / ketosis hypothesis** _(Brian J. Ford)_

Severe alcoholism or starvation produces elevated blood acetone, in principle slightly lowering ignition temperature; suggested as a contributing factor rather than a cause.

### Skeptical

**Conventional fire with omitted ignition source** _(Joe Nickell, John F. Fischer, Benjamin Radford)_

Reported SHC cases are conventional house or armchair fires whose ignition source (cigarette, hearth ember, candle, electric heater) is either lost in the burn or omitted from the published narrative.
